\section{Introduction}
Dropout is used to ``corrupt'' a network so that it relies less on particular paths from input to output \cite{srivastava2014dropout}. Keeping the average dropout fixed, where should we introduce this corruption to make it most useful? We study this question near the edge of chaos, asking whether redistributing dropout across layers can preserve information propagation while exposing more of the network to regularization \cite{poole2016exponentialexpressivitydeepneural,schoenholz2017deepinformationpropagation,Bahri2020StatMechDeepLearning,bahri2024leshoucheslecturesdeep}.

In standard mean-field theory, driving a neural network to criticality means delaying the approach to perfect correlation that can result from passing distinct inputs through an identical network. Indistinguishable representations at the output layer leave no information to tell the inputs apart, and thus, avoiding this is paramount to ensure proper performance in neural networks. However, the presence of dropout causes the perfect-correlation fixed point to evaporate \cite{schoenholz2017deepinformationpropagation}, as even identical inputs see different representations due to the independent masks blocking a percentage of activations at a given layer. Correlation lengths derived from mean-field theory serve as an effective decay scale, much like a half-life for information, and are instrumental in model building, as networks much deeper than this length can be difficult to train. But if dropout removes the perfect-correlation fixed point, where do correlations settle instead, and how many layers does it take to get there? In this work, we derive what this fixed point is using a mean-field theory computation perturbative in dropout, and use it to determine the effective correlation length, showing that this actually depends on a combination of distance to criticality and dropout.

In the representation-group (coarse-graining) language of \cite{roberts2022principlesdeeplearningtheory,bahri2024leshoucheslecturesdeep}, dropout is a relevant perturbation: its effect grows under depth coarse-graining and moves the dynamics away from criticality. The depth correlation length is then finite even at the clean network's edge of chaos \cite{sompolinsky1988chaos,packard1988adaptation,bertschinger2004real}. Dropout can reduce overfitting while shortening the distance over which information propagates, and we use the propagation theory to guide where it is applied, then test whether this improves performance over uniform dropout.


Expanding the correlation map near perfect alignment gives an equation for the decorrelation $m \equiv 1-c^\ast$, where $c^\ast$ is the asymptotic inter-signal correlation. The leading nonlinear term depends on the activation: smooth and kinked (\relu{}-like) activations have the same qualitative phase diagram but different power laws in the presence of dropout.

We also show that changing dropout strength and distance to criticality gives curves that collapse onto a universal form within each activation class, and compare this prediction with mean-field recursions.

While dropout was previously shown to destroy the order-to-chaos critical point \cite{schoenholz2017deepinformationpropagation}, we show that the deformed mean-field map still retains a nontrivial $c^\ast<1$ fixed point. This allows us to have a well-defined correlation length that can guide architecture depth. We show that the analytic structure of activations sets the universality classes and the scaling laws for information propagation: smooth and kinked activations have distinct critical exponents (Table~\ref{tab:critical_exponents}), a distinction reflected in their Hermite spectral structure (App.~\ref{sec:var-opt-act}). Since the same Gaussian activation kernels arise beyond MLPs, including CNNs and residual branches (App.~\ref{app:cnn_extension}), we expect the smooth/kinked distinction to help guide these cases too. We also treat dropout as a field that varies with depth, allowing a hyperparameter usually held constant to be optimized across the network, and potentially over training time using dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) \cite{bordelon2022selfconsistent}.

This work complements prior studies of depth-dependent regularization: stochastic depth drops whole residual blocks with increasing probability \cite{huang2016deep}, curriculum dropout anneals dropout over training time \cite{morerio2017curriculum}, and LayerDrop removes transformer layers for efficiency \cite{fan2019reducing}. We vary dropout across depth, so our schedules can be combined with changes over training time and with the removal of whole layers. Qualitatively different edge-of-chaos behavior for smooth and \relu{}-like activations was previously observed in \cite{hayou2019impact}; here we extract the scaling exponents and identify the corresponding universality classes.\footnote{The notebooks, saved results and confidence interval calculations are available in the \href{https://github.com/luklacasito/icml}{\texttt{icml}} repository.
