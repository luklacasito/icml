\section{Universality Classes and Critical Scaling under Dropout}

The following analysis provides the mathematical basis for our scheduling argument, with results on critical scaling and universality that may be of particular interest to physicists. Readers primarily interested in experimental performance can skip directly to Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}.

Dropout \cite{srivastava2014dropout,wager2013dropout} is often used as a regularizer that reduces overfitting by randomly masking activations during training, thereby reducing the network's reliance on any single subset of pathways and discouraging co-adaptation. In the previous literature, dropout was explored in MFT \cite{lee2019mean, schoenholz2017deepinformationpropagation}, but not in the universality class sense. We apply dropout after the activation as multiplicative Bernoulli noise,
\begin{equation}
p^{(\ell)}_{j} \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho), \qquad y^{(\ell)}_j \;\mapsto\; \tilde y^{(\ell)}_j \equiv \frac{p^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\rho} y^{(\ell)}_j,
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the standard inverted-dropout convention in which $\mathbb E_p[\tilde y^{(\ell)}_j]=y^{(\ell)}_j$, and hence $\mathbb E_p[\tilde z^{\ell+1}_i\mid W,b,y^\ell]=z^{\ell+1}_i$, so the mean preactivation is invariant to the dropout rate \cite{schoenholz2017deepinformationpropagation}. Throughout, $\rho$ is the keep probability. When comparing two inputs, we draw the dropout masks independently for each input\footnote{If the two inputs share a mask, the same coordinates are removed, so identical representations remain identical and $c=1$ is preserved. Here we consider independently sampled masks.}; this independence is the source of the decorrelation effect analyzed below.

The smooth/kinked distinction also appears in the Hermite expansion of the activation: $\tanh$ has rapidly decaying coefficients, while \relu{} has a power-law tail. These coefficients determine which derivatives of the correlation map remain finite near perfect alignment, connecting activation regularity to the dropout scaling laws (App.~\ref{sec:var-opt-act}).

Our classification differs from the distinction between scale-invariant and non-scale-invariant activations in \cite{roberts2022principlesdeeplearningtheory}: the decay of the Hermite coefficients depends primarily on analytic structure rather than scale invariance. We distinguish the two classes because (a) near-critical scaling laws differ between smooth and kinked activations, and (b) ordinary kinks produce power-law Hermite tails, whereas the smooth examples considered here have much faster decay (Apps.~\ref{ap:exponent_derivations} and~\ref{sec:var-opt-act}).

Consider sending exactly the same input through the network twice. Without dropout, its representations remain identical at every layer, whereas independent masks remove different coordinates on the two passes and break this alignment. Rescaling the surviving activations preserves their mean over masks, but does not restore the agreement between the two representations. Perfect alignment therefore ceases to be a fixed point, cutting off the divergence of the depth correlation length at the edge \cite{schoenholz2017deepinformationpropagation}. For a single input, the corresponding variance increase can instead be absorbed into an effective weight variance, $\sigma_w^2\mapsto\sigma_w^2/\rho$.

With keep probability $\rho$, the preactivations become
\begin{equation}
z_i^\ell = \frac{1}{\rho} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\ell-1}} W_{ij}^\ell p_j^{\ell-1} y_j^{\ell-1} + b_i^\ell.
\end{equation}
The single-input variance recursion becomes
\begin{equation}
\bar{q}_{aa}^{\ell} = \frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{\rho} \int Dz \phi^{2}\left( \sqrt{\bar{q}_{aa}^{\ell-1}} z \right) + \sigma_{b}^{2},
\end{equation}
where the bar denotes the variance with dropout. A fixed point, if it exists, satisfies
\begin{equation}
\bar q^{*} \;=\; \frac{\sigma_w^2}{\rho}\int Dz \phi^2\big(\sqrt{\bar q^{*}} z\big) + \sigma_b^2.
\label{eq:qstar-dropout}
\end{equation}
Assuming a positive fixed point $\bar q^*$, we can evaluate the correlation map at perfect alignment to find how far independent masks move the representations away from $c=1$. For two distinct inputs $a\neq b$ with $c^{\ell}_{ab}=1$, we obtain \cite{schoenholz2017deepinformationpropagation}
\begin{equation}
\bar F_\rho(1)= \bar c^{\ell+1}_{ab} = 1 - \frac{1 - \rho}{\rho \bar{q}^{*}} \sigma_{w}^{2} \int Dz \phi^{2}\left( \sqrt{\bar{q}^{*}} z \right).
\end{equation}
We call this departure from perfect alignment the dropout field
\begin{align}
h &\;\equiv\; 1-\bar F_\rho(1)\nonumber\\
&\;=\; \frac{1-\rho}{\rho \bar q^{*}}\; \sigma_w^2\int Dz \phi^2\big(\sqrt{\bar q^{*}} z\big).
\label{eq:Delta_exact}
\end{align}
For a nondegenerate channel with $\sigma_w^2\int Dz\,\phi(\sqrt{\bar q^*}z)^2>0$, construction gives $\rho<1\Rightarrow h>0$,
so the fixed point at $c^*=1$ is immediately spoiled: even if two inputs are perfectly aligned at some depth, the next layer produces $\bar c_{ab}=1-h<1$. For weak dropout, writing $\delta\rho\equiv 1-\rho$ with $\delta\rho\ll 1$, suppose the no-dropout variance fixed point is positive, isolated and stable, with variance-map derivative $|\chi_q|<1$. The implicit-function theorem then gives $\bar q^*=q^*+\mathcal O(\delta\rho)$, so to leading order
\begin{equation}
h = \delta\rho\; \frac{\sigma_w^2}{\bar q^{*}}\int Dz \phi^2\big(\sqrt{\bar q^{*}} z\big) + \mathcal O(\delta\rho^2).
\label{eq:Delta_weakdrop}
\end{equation}
This variance expansion excludes a marginal variance fixed point. For bias-free \relu{} at unchanged He initialization, dropout gives $q_{\ell+1}=q_\ell/\rho$; its normalized correlation map can still be analyzed without a converged variance, as in App.~\ref{sec:kinks}.
The backward pass has the same source of asymmetry. Let \(\delta^\ell_{j,a}\) be the backpropagated preactivation gradient for input \(a\). With inverted dropout,
\begin{equation}
\delta^\ell_{j,a} = \frac{p^\ell_{j,a}}{\rho} \phi'(z^\ell_{j,a}) \sum_i W^{\ell+1}_{ij}\delta^{\ell+1}_{i,a}.
\end{equation}
For equal hidden-layer widths, let \(G^\ell_{ab}\equiv \mathbb E[\delta^\ell_{j,a}\delta^\ell_{j,b}]\) denote the covariance per coordinate. Under the usual gradient-independence approximation at infinite width, the recursion contains
\begin{equation}
G^\ell_{ab} = \sigma_w^2 \mathbb E\left[ \frac{p^\ell_a p^\ell_b}{\rho^2} \phi'(u_a)\phi'(u_b) \right] G^{\ell+1}_{ab}.
\end{equation}
Since $p_a^2=p_a$, the mask contributes
\[
\frac{\mathbb E[p_a^2]}{\rho^2}=\frac1\rho,
\qquad
\frac{\mathbb E[p_ap_b]}{\rho^2}=1\quad(a\ne b).
\]
Within the gradient-independence approximation, the variance therefore receives an extra factor that is absent from the covariance between different inputs. After normalization, this suggests a loss of gradient alignment, described by a backward field $h_\nabla(\rho)$. Its scaling requires a separate calculation: the backward kernel contains $\phi'$, which is discontinuous for \relu{}, so we cannot simply transfer the forward result.

\subsection{Activation Regularity and Critical Exponents}

With masks drawn independently across units and inputs, we measure the fixed point's departure from perfect correlation by $m\equiv1-c^*$, and its detuning from the correlation edge by $t\equiv\chi_\rho-1$, where $\chi_\rho\equiv\bar F_\rho'(1)$ is the slope at perfect alignment. The dropout field $h$ is defined in \eqref{eq:Delta_exact}. For smooth activations, Price's theorem gives
\begin{align}
\chi_\rho &= \sigma_w^2\int Dz \big[\phi'(\sqrt{\bar q^{*}} z)\big]^2,
\label{eq:chi_dropout}\\
g_\rho &\equiv \bar F_\rho''(1) = \sigma_w^2 \bar q^{*}\int Dz \big[\phi''(\sqrt{\bar q^{*}} z)\big]^2.
\label{eq:g_dropout}
\end{align}
We assume the derivative identities hold through third order, with $\phi',\phi'',\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\in L^2(\mathcal N(0,\bar q^*))$, and that $\bar q^*$ and $g_\rho$ have finite positive limits near the reference edge (App.~\ref{app:price_dropout}). Expanding the fixed-point condition $1-m=\bar F_\rho(1-m)$ then gives, to leading order,
\begin{equation}
h = \frac{g_\rho}{2}m^2-tm.
\label{eq:EoS_correct}
\end{equation}
The omitted term is $\mathcal O(m^3)$; when $g_\rho=0$, as for a linear activation, this smooth-class balance does not apply.

For \relu{}, imagine two nearly identical signals arriving on opposite sides of zero: one passes through, while the other is clipped. At a fixed nonzero Gaussian variance and correlation $c$ close to one, the signals have a root-mean-square separation $\mathcal O(\sqrt{1-c})$, so a fraction $\mathcal O(\sqrt{1-c})$ can straddle the kink. Within this small region, their mean squared activation difference is $\mathcal O(1-c)$, giving a contribution $\mathcal O((1-c)^{3/2})$. A continuous, piecewise $C^2$ activation with a finite slope jump and locally bounded, Gaussian-integrable regular second derivative therefore has the leading balance
\begin{equation}
h=\kappa m^{3/2}-tm+\mathcal O(m^2),
\label{eq:eos_kink_again}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa>0$ is the kink coefficient; for the normalized bias-free \relu{} map at the zero-dropout edge, $\kappa=2\sqrt{2}/(3\pi)$ (App.~\ref{app:tube_argument_kink}). At $t=0$, the two balances give $m\sim h^{1/2}$ for smooth activations and $m\sim h^{2/3}$ for kinked activations.

To see what this changes in propagation, let $\lambda\equiv\bar F_\rho'(c^*)$ be the slope at the displaced fixed point. For $0<\lambda<1$, small correlation perturbations decay over the depth scale $\xi_{c,\rho}\equiv-1/\log\lambda$. At $t=0$, linearizing the two maps gives
\begin{equation}
\xi_{c,\rho}(0,h)\sim
\begin{cases}
h^{-1/2}, & \text{smooth activations},\\
h^{-1/3}, & \text{kinked activations}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Independent dropout therefore makes the propagation length finite at the edge, and whether the activation is smooth or kinked determines how this length depends on dropout. It measures how quickly the network loses sensitivity to input correlations at initialization, which we use below to compare dropout schedules.

Table~\ref{tab:critical_exponents} collects our exponents: $\xi(t,0)\sim|t|^{-\nu_t}$, $\xi(0,h)\sim h^{-\nu_\rho}$, $m(t,0)\sim t^\beta$ for $t>0$, $m(0,h)\sim h^{1/\delta}$, and $\left.\partial_hm\right|_{h\to0}\sim|t|^{-\gamma}$, with $\xi$ denoting $\xi_{c,\rho}$. At $t=h=0$, $m_\ell\equiv1-c_\ell\sim\ell^{-\theta_{\rm rel}}$ defines the relaxation exponent. For completeness, $\alpha$ describes $f_{\rm sing,on}(t,0)\sim t^{2-\alpha}$ as $t\to0^+$, where $f_{\rm sing,on}$ is the singular stationary value of a potential obtained by integrating the equation of state. App.~\ref{ap:exponent_derivations} gives the definitions and derivations.

\subsection{Scaling collapse}
Changing dropout changes both the decorrelation and the distance from criticality at which dropout becomes important. The equation of state tells us how to rescale these quantities so that curves obtained at different dropout strengths coincide near criticality. For smooth activations, we define
\begin{equation}
\tilde m \equiv m \sqrt{\frac{g_\rho}{2h}}, \qquad \tilde t \equiv -\frac{t}{\sqrt{2g_\rho h}}.
\label{eq:rescaled_vars_smooth}
\end{equation}
giving
\begin{equation}
\tilde m = \sqrt{1+\tilde t^{2}}-\tilde t.
\label{eq:universal_scaling_function_smooth_correct}
\end{equation}
The leading equation therefore gives the same curve at every dropout strength \cite{ZinnJustin2002QFTCritical}, which the full correlation map approaches near criticality (Fig.~\ref{fig:uni_scaling}). The crossover occurs at $|t|\sim\sqrt{g_\rho h}$ for smooth activations and $|t|\sim\kappa^{2/3}h^{1/3}$ for kinked activations, separating the region where dropout sets the decorrelation from the region where distance to criticality matters more. App.~\ref{app:crossover_details} gives both scaling functions and their limiting branches.

For ordinary bias-free \relu{}, the microscopic dropout path locks $t=-h$, so dropout controls the leading response as $h\to0$, without allowing us to vary the two parameters independently along $t=0,h>0$. Higher-order terms in the map and changes in the kink coefficient cause the curves to depart from the leading prediction at larger fields. These crossover scales indicate how close an initialization must be to the edge before dropout controls its propagation length; they do not by themselves predict trained performance.

\subsection{Optimal Dropout Scheduling and Regularization Reach}
\label{subsec:optimal_dropout}

Thus far, we have considered only homogeneous dropout schedules, where dropout is treated as a constant hyperparameter in depth. We now seek profiles that will keep or improve the regularization, while aiding information propagation through longer correlation lengths. Let us introduce a depth-dependent keep probability $\rho_\ell$ for the mask applied after the activation at layer $\ell$ and define the associated dropout field
\begin{equation}
h_\ell \;\equiv\; 1-\bar F_{\rho_\ell}(1),
\end{equation}
i.e. the shift of the correlation map at perfect alignment induced by keep probability $\rho_\ell$. If variances have settled so that a scalar map applies at each layer, the exact linear response along the trajectory $c_{\ell+1}=\bar F_{\rho_\ell}(c_\ell)$ is
\begin{equation}
\delta c_{L+1}=\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^L \bar F_{\rho_\ell}'(c_\ell)\right]\delta c_1.
\end{equation}
To compare schedules, we approximate each derivative by its value at the local fixed point and hold the near-critical coefficients $t$ and $g_\rho$ at the same reference values. In the smooth class it follows that (see \eqref{eq:lambda_smooth_correct})
\begin{equation}
\lambda(t,h_\ell)\;\simeq\; 1-\sqrt{t^2+2g_\rho h_\ell}.
\end{equation}
For $0<\lambda_\ell\lesssim 1$, this approximates the propagated perturbation by
\begin{equation}
\delta c_{L+1} \;\sim\; \prod_{\ell=1}^L \lambda_\ell \delta c_1, \qquad \lambda_\ell \;\equiv\; \lambda(t,h_\ell),
\label{delta_c_dropout_perturb}
\end{equation}
so we define the effective correlation length as
\begin{equation}
\xi_{\rm eff}^{-1} \;\equiv\; -\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L \log\lambda_\ell \;\simeq\; \frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L \sqrt{t^2+2g_\rho h_\ell}.
\end{equation}
At the correlation edge $t=0$, this reduces to
\begin{equation}
\xi_{\rm eff}^{-1}(t=0) \;\simeq\; \sqrt{2g_\rho} \frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L h_\ell^{1/2} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L h_\ell^{1/2}.
\label{eff_corr}
\end{equation}
Fix $\bar h=L^{-1}\sum_\ell h_\ell$ and $0\le h_\ell\le h_{\max}$, with $0<\bar h\le h_{\max}$. Jensen's inequality and the chord bound give
\begin{equation}
\frac{\bar h}{\sqrt{h_{\max}}}
\le \frac1L\sum_{\ell=1}^L\sqrt{h_\ell}
\le \sqrt{\bar h}.
\end{equation}
Uniform dropout therefore maximizes the leading-order decay rate and minimizes $\xi_{\rm eff}$. In the continuum, the minimum decay rate uses only $0$ and $h_{\max}$, with active fraction $f=\bar h/h_{\max}$. Its position is unrestricted; regularization reach below selects the input side:
\begin{equation}
h(x)=\begin{cases}h_{\max},&0\le x\le f,\\
0,&f<x\le1,\end{cases}
\qquad x=\ell/L.
\end{equation}
For finite $L$, at most one layer remains partially filled. The field budget agrees with a dropout-probability budget to leading order in weak dropout, by \eqref{eq:Delta_weakdrop}. These conclusions assume weak fields and local fixed points; abrupt schedules need not preserve this approximation.

Comparing this step profile with uniform dropout in the continuum, to leading order uniform dropout yields $\xi_{\rm eff}\sim \bar h^{-1/2}$ while the step schedule gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\xi_{\rm eff,step}}{\xi_{\rm eff,uniform}} = \sqrt{\frac{h_{\max}}{\bar h}} \;\ge\; 1.
\end{equation}
For kinked activations at $t=0$, the same local approximation gives $\xi_{\rm eff}^{-1}\propto L^{-1}\sum_\ell h_\ell^{1/3}$. Jensen's inequality and the chord bound now give
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\bar h}{h_{\max}^{2/3}}
\le \frac1L\sum_{\ell=1}^L h_\ell^{1/3}
\le \bar h^{1/3}.
\end{equation*}
Concentrating dropout therefore again maximizes the approximate $\xi_{\rm eff}$, with at most one partially filled layer; regularization reach selects the earliest layers.

As an exercise to intuitively understand why this schedule makes sense without the need for MFT machinery, we consider the following thought experiment: dropout is used to ``corrupt'' the network so as to prevent it from memorizing certain ``paths'' from input to output. A toy model for understanding how to best corrupt every part of the network with a set amount of damage is to imagine a similar system: a river where a set amount of ink is to be distributed with the goal of corrupting every cross section as much as possible. Reframed this way, it is obvious that front-loading and letting the current propagate the ink forward is the way to go. While this serves as an intuitive explanation, the following regularization reach argument makes this intuition explicit mathematically.

Let us normalize depth $x=\ell/L$ and write $j(x)=h(x)/\bar h$ for the injection density, with $\bar h>0$ and $\int_0^1j(x)\,dx=1$. For the river in Fig.~\ref{fig:river_reach}, where the ink does not fade as it travels downstream, the accumulated exposure at $x$ and its average along the river are
\begin{align}
E(x)&=\int_0^x j(s)\,ds,\\
\bar E&=\int_0^1 E(x)\,dx
       =\int_0^1(1-s)j(s)\,ds.
\end{align}
Uniform injection has $j=1$ throughout; early and late injection have $j=2$ over their respective halves. Thus
\begin{equation}
\bar E_{\rm early}=\tfrac34,\qquad
\bar E_{\rm uniform}=\tfrac12,\qquad
\bar E_{\rm late}=\tfrac14.
\end{equation}
All three schedules deliver the same total ink to the outlet, but expose different amounts of the river along the way.

An early mask affects many later layers, even as its influence fades; a late mask has fewer layers left to regularize. To quantify this, compare the same signal with and without a mask at layer $\ell$, writing $c_k$ for their normalized second-moment overlap at layer $k$ and $\eta_k=1-c_k$. Inverted dropout preserves the cross moment but multiplies the masked second moment by $1/\rho_\ell$, so
\begin{equation}
\eta_\ell=1-\sqrt{\rho_\ell}
=\frac{1-\rho_\ell}{2}+\mathcal O((1-\rho_\ell)^2).
\end{equation}
By \eqref{eq:Delta_weakdrop}, this is $\eta_\ell\simeq C h_\ell$ for a constant $C>0$. Neglecting variance transients, linearizing the ordered correlation map with $\lambda=F'(1)\in(0,1)$ gives
\begin{align}
\eta_{k+1}&\simeq\lambda\eta_k,\\
\eta_{\ell+r}&\simeq\lambda^r\eta_\ell
\simeq C h_\ell e^{-r/\xi_c},\qquad
\xi_c=-\frac{1}{\log\lambda}.
\end{align}
Holding the same background, $C$ and $\xi_c$ fixed across mask positions, we add this influence over the remaining layers to define the exposure:
\begin{align}
\mathcal D_\ell\equiv\sum_{r=1}^{L-\ell}\eta_{\ell+r}
&\simeq C h_\ell\sum_{r=1}^{L-\ell}e^{-r/\xi_c}\nonumber\\
&\approx C h_\ell\xi_c\left(1-e^{-(L-\ell)/\xi_c}\right)\nonumber\\
&\equiv C h_\ell w_\ell.
\label{eq:regularization_reach}
\end{align}
The second line replaces the sum by its continuum integral. Since $w_\ell$ decreases with depth, moving dropout to earlier layers increases the summed exposure $\sum_\ell h_\ell w_\ell$. At fixed budget and per-layer bounds, its maximum fills the earliest layers first. Adding mask effects in this way is our regularization heuristic; whether the resulting schedule improves generalization is tested experimentally. App.~\ref{app:regularization_reach} gives further details and the limits of the approximation.
